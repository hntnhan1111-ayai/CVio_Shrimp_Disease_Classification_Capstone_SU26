1.2 Project Overview

screening from images. The project contains two formal AI tracks. The classification track
predicts Healthy, Black Gill disease (BG), White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV), or the WSSV_BG

2. Project Background
Shrimp aquaculture is economically important, but disease outbreaks can cause substantial
production loss and instability. Existing disease recognition may depend on farmer experience,
visual inspection, laboratory diagnosis, or veterinary expertise. Laboratory methods are more
reliable than image-based screening, but they may be unavailable, delayed, or costly in resource-
constrained settings. A mobile image-analysis system can therefore support rapid preliminary
screening, provided that its limitations are stated clearly.
Shrimp disease images are difficult because symptoms can be small, sparse, visually overlapping,
or affected by lighting, blur, noise, compression, reflections, pose variation, and background clutter.
The WSSV_BG co-infection class is particularly challenging because it can contain evidence shared
with both BG and WSSV . Instance segmentation is harder than classification because it must identify
both the disease category and the spatial location of visible symptoms. Background removal can
reduce irrelevant cues for classification, but it is not automatically beneficial for segmentation
because contextual boundaries and local disease regions may be altered or removed.
4
4\n\n--- PAGE 13 ---\nThe project also addresses evaluation validity. Several images may show repeated views of the
same physical shrimp. Random image-level splitting can therefore place views of one specimen
in both training and testing partitions, creating leakage and inflated localization metrics. The final
segmentation protocol uses specimen-grouped splitting to reduce this risk.
3. Project Objective
The main objective is to design, evaluate, document, and deploy a lightweight deep-learning
system for shrimp disease screening that improves research performance while remaining suitable
for mobile-oriented use. The measurable objectives are as follows:
1. Organize and preprocess public shrimp disease image datasets using fixed and traceable data
protocols.
2. Benchmark representative TIMM and official Ultralytics YOLO classification models and
justify the selection of YOLO26m-cls under a parameter budget below approximately 15
million parameters.
3. Improve the selected YOLO26m-cls backbone using ASL-LDAM and SimAM-DCFR, with
Macro-F1 as the primary classification metric.
4. Evaluate the selected classifier on clean test data, controlled corruption conditions,
explainable-AI visualizations, ShrimpDB-3, and Combined-4 experiments.
5. Create BG and WSSV disease-region masks for instance segmentation while retaining Healthy
images as empty-label negative samples.
6. Apply a specimen-grouped split protocol and quantify the effect of leakage-prone image-level
splitting.
7. Evaluate attention-based and Fourier-domain segmentation improvements against controlled
baselines.
8. Convert and integrate the selected classification model into a native Android application using
TensorFlow Lite.
9. Implement user and administrator workflows with authentication, camera/gallery input,
prediction display, mask display, history, export/delete operations, and Firebase integration.
10. Maintain reproducible datasets, configurations, checkpoints, metrics, figures, and thesis
evidence through controlled research-asset management.
4. Problem Statement
The project addresses the problem of developing a lightweight shrimp disease image-analysis
system that balances class-balanced classification performance, disease-region localization, robust-
ness, model efficiency, reproducibility, and mobile deployment feasibility. A model that performs
well only on a clean image split may still fail under corruption, depend on background cues, or
produce optimistic results because of repeated-specimen leakage. Conversely, a highly accurate but
large model may be unsuitable for mobile inference.
The research problem is therefore not limited to maximizing a single accuracy value. It
requires a controlled model-selection process, a class-balanced primary metric, consistent baseline
5
5\n\n--- PAGE 14 ---\ncomparison, explicit segmentation annotation semantics, leakage-aware evaluation, and practical
integration constraints. The application must be presented as preliminary screening rather than
clinical diagnosis, and reported conclusions must remain bounded by the available datasets and
experimental protocols.
5. Significance of the Project
The project has both scientific and practical significance. Scientifically, it combines image-level
classification and disease-region instance segmentation within one shrimp disease study. The
classification branch supports rapid screening, while segmentation provides spatial evidence and
enables stricter analysis of false positives, missed disease regions, repeated-specimen leakage,
attention mechanisms, and Fourier-domain transformations. The model-selection study also
compares TIMM and YOLO candidates under model-efficiency constraints rather than selecting
a backbone without evidence.
Practically, the project demonstrates how the selected model can be converted and integrated into
an Android application for farmers and administrators. The system supports image capture or upload,
prediction display, optional visual evidence, inspection history, and Firebase-backed management.
This integration does not prove farm-ready deployment, but it provides a traceable application path
from research artifacts to a working prototype.

6.2 Project Limitations
Dataset coverage and external validity are limited. Most experiments use ShrimpDiseaseIm-
ageBD, with ShrimpDB-3 and Combined-4 providing additional evidence. These datasets do not
represent every farm, geographic region, camera, lighting condition, water environment, disease
stage, or shrimp population. Consequently, the reported results demonstrate performance under the
documented datasets and splits, not universal field generalization.
The instance segmentation labels have annotation limitations. BG and WSSV masks were
produced by one annotator to preserve labeling consistency, but no independent aquatic-animal-
health expert or second annotator reviewed the masks. Disease boundaries can be ambiguous,
7
7\n\n--- PAGE 16 ---\nespecially in co-infection images and low-quality photographs. The segmentation results must
therefore be interpreted relative to this single-annotator protocol.
The evaluation protocol reduces, but cannot eliminate, all sources of bias. Specimen-grouped
splitting was introduced to reduce repeated-view leakage; however, specimen identity depends on
the available filename and grouping information. Robustness evaluation uses a finite set of task-
appropriate corruptions and does not reproduce every real acquisition disturbance. Background
removal is used for classification but is not assumed to be suitable for segmentation because it may
remove localization-relevant context.
Explainability and deployment evidence also have restricted scope. Heatmap-based XAI is
qualitative and indicates influential image regions, but it does not establish causality or clinical
validity. The Android application demonstrates model integration, authentication, prediction
display, history, mask visualization, Firebase services, and administrator functions, but it has not
undergone formal farmer user-acceptance testing and is not guaranteed to provide real-time inference
on every mobile device.
The system is intended only for preliminary image-based screening and research demonstration.
It does not replace laboratory testing, veterinary assessment, treatment prescription, or autonomous
clinical decision-making. These limitations define the valid interpretation of the findings and
motivate future work involving broader farm data, multi-annotator mask review, device-level
benchmarking, and prospective user evaluation.

Zalo is used for immediate coordination because technical blockers do not always occur
near the scheduled weekly meeting. A blocker may concern dataset access, an invalid label, a
failed training run, incompatible model operators, unexpected metric changes, Firebase behavior,
document formatting, or dependency delays. The responsible member reports the issue as soon as
it is identified, and the project leader determines whether it can be resolved directly or requires
supervisor discussion.
Escalation is proportionate to impact. A local implementation defect can be corrected without
changing the project plan if it does not alter the dataset, metric, comparison protocol, or deadline. A
blocker that changes a model configuration, invalidates a result, affects another member, or threatens
a milestone is raised in Google Meet and recorded. This distinction prevents both extremes: silently
changing important experimental conditions and over-formalizing minor technical corrections.

important because management decisions and scientific claims must describe the same project state.

2.1.1 WBS Design Principles
The WBS decomposes the project by deliverable and research responsibility rather than by
generic development activity. Parent work packages represent planning and governance, clas-
sification data, baseline benchmarking, proposed classification, segmentation data, segmentation
modeling, mobile integration, and final documentation and closure. Child tasks are sufficiently
specific to assign an owner and define an output, but they do not descend into trivial operations that
would make the schedule unreadable.
Research tasks are separated when they have different acceptance conditions. Literature
review, implementation, controlled training, ablation, robustness evaluation, external evaluation,
qualitative interpretation, and reproducibility packaging are not treated as one combined task. This
decomposition makes it possible to identify where a claim originated and whether the required
supporting evidence was actually completed.

One man-day is defined as eight hours for estimation. Because the project is a capstone rather
than contracted employment, the estimate is used as a workload and scheduling measure, not as
payroll accounting. Work may occur on any day of the week, and the team is evaluated by completion

effort and deadlines without claiming a conventional five-day employment pattern.

checkpoint handling, failure recovery, and result extraction. This does not imply that unattended

project time and affects dependencies. The 487 man-day total is consequently a planning aggregate
that combines active work and allocated experiment completion time.
2.1.3 Dependency and Parallel-Execution Logic
Dependencies protect scientific validity. Classification model comparison depends on a
fixed dataset split and consistent metrics. Proposed-method experiments depend on the selected

comparisons depend on the same baseline protocol. Mobile inference depends on a validated model
conversion and stable class order. Documentation depends on results that have passed consistency
checks.
Parallelism is used when dependencies permit it. Android UI and application infrastructure begin
early while model research continues. Attention and Fourier segmentation are developed by different
members after the common dataset preparation becomes available. Classification paper writing
begins before all final thesis sections are integrated, while reproducibility packaging overlaps late
experiments. This controlled parallelism shortens the project calendar without treating dependent
outputs as interchangeable.

Risk identification is performed from the perspective of research validity, delivery feasibility,
and claim defensibility. A risk is considered critical when it can invalidate the comparison, create
leakage, disconnect a reported metric from its checkpoint, or cause the thesis to make a claim
unsupported by the available evidence. Operational inconvenience alone is not automatically critical
unless it threatens a milestone or prevents completion of a required workstream.
Impact and possibility are assessed qualitatively because the project does not have sufficient
historical data for calibrated probabilities. The classifications are used to focus attention, assign
preventive controls, and determine escalation. High-impact risks are reviewed during weekly
meetings, while immediate failures are reported through Zalo. The risk table records the agreed
response rather than pretending that all uncertainty can be eliminated.
2.2.2 Scientific-Validity Risks
Repeated-specimen leakage is a major segmentation risk because visually related views of the
same shrimp can inflate test performance when divided across partitions. The grouped-specimen
split and explicit overlap audit reduce this threat. The project still reports the limitation that specimen
identifiers are inferred from available naming structure and dataset evidence. Grouping improves
validity but does not guarantee that every hidden source correlation has been removed.
Single-annotator masks improve internal labeling consistency but create uncertainty about
clinical correctness and boundary subjectivity. The project addresses this by documenting mask
semantics, using one protocol, treating Healthy images as empty-label negatives, and disclosing the
absence of independent expert review. The limitation is not hidden by the use of precise polygon
format or model metrics. Similar caution applies to XAI, which provides qualitative model-attention
evidence but not causal or veterinary proof.
Inconsistent experimental settings are controlled because they can invalidate baseline compar-
isons more directly than a small metric difference. Dataset version, split, seed, preprocessing,
augmentation, optimizer, checkpoint selection, class order, and evaluation code must be fixed or
explicitly identified when comparing methods. The final classification result of 91.01% Macro-F1
is required to be reported consistently in the final document according to the accepted project
consolidation, and related tables, figures, and conclusions must use the same interpretation.
2.2.3 Resource, Integration, and Schedule Risks
Kaggle GPU availability, long training time, failed runs, and storage limits can delay research.
The response is to debug with staged experiments, preserve checkpoints, prioritize the experiments
necessary for the declared contributions, and revise deadlines through the weekly process. The
project avoids presenting every attempted run as equally important; compute is allocated first to
matched baselines, proposed methods, ablations, and required validation.
Mobile deployment introduces conversion and compatibility risks. Unsupported operators,
changed tensor order, preprocessing mismatch, class-order mismatch, memory use, and CPU latency
can make a high-performing training checkpoint unusable in the application. The team therefore
validates TensorFlow Lite inputs and outputs, reports measured behavior, and avoids an unsupported
17

interruptions do not redefine the model result.
2.2.4 Risk Ownership, Residual Risk, and Escalation
The operational owner of a workstream is responsible for detecting and reporting its risks, but
the response may require multiple roles. Phong owns labeling, grouped-split, leakage, and Fourier
risks; Nhu owns mobile, Firebase, UI, mask-display, and attention-integration risks; Nhan owns

rather than an undocumented folder state.
Segmentation quality requires additional controls because the project creates new labels. Mask
semantics define what visible BG and WSSV evidence is included and excluded. Polygon
conversion must be accepted by the training parser, image-mask correspondence must remain valid,
and Healthy images must contain no disease mask. Grouped splitting is audited for specimen overlap.
The single-annotator process is retained for consistency, while the lack of expert review is treated
as a limitation rather than a completed quality gate.
2.3.2 Experimental and Model-Comparison Quality
Classification comparisons are governed by a fixed seed-42 protocol, consistent image partitions,
documented preprocessing, and a common evaluation script. Macro-F1 is the primary ranking
measure because the four-class problem is imbalanced and includes a co-infection class. Accuracy,
classwise scores, Kappa, parameters, size, latency, FPS, corruption results, and XAI may support
interpretation, but they do not replace the declared primary selection rule.

does not establish novelty. The thesis must distinguish adopted components, team modifications,
reimplementations, and newly proposed combinations. Unsupported causal interpretations and
claims that exceed the dataset or protocol are removed during quality review.

through Zalo is temporary and cannot be the only location of an accepted result.
Artifact review also checks internal consistency. The metric shown in a table must match the
associated figure and discussion. Model names must not change meaning between methodology
and results. Healthy must be described as a classification class and an empty-label segmentation
negative, not as a segmentation mask class. ShrimpXNet must remain a literature reference
for classification, whereas the proposed classification method is built directly on the selected
YOLO26m-cls engineering baseline.

1. Overview of the Field
Shrimp aquaculture has repeatedly been affected by viral, bacterial, parasitic, and environmen-
tally mediated disease events. Historical reviews describe how pathogens such as WSSV , IHHNV ,
YHV , and related syndromes caused severe production losses and forced the industry to adopt
stronger biosecurity, surveillance, and health-management practices [1–9]. These studies establish
the practical motivation for rapid screening, but they also show why image analysis cannot replace
laboratory confirmation: multiple diseases can share visible symptoms, signs may appear only at
particular infection stages, and environmental stress can produce visually similar abnormalities.
Computer vision provides a complementary, non-invasive route for preliminary screening. In the
broader smart-aquaculture literature, deep learning has been applied to species recognition, biomass
estimation, behavior monitoring, water-quality support, and disease-oriented image analysis [10].
For shrimp disease research, the most relevant tasks are image classification and instance segmen-
tation. Classification assigns an image-level label and is appropriate for fast mobile screening.
Instance segmentation predicts a separate mask for each visible disease region and therefore supports
spatial evidence, stricter localization evaluation, and investigation of small or irregular lesions.
The literature is fragmented across several technical directions. Shrimp-specific studies often
optimize clean-test classification accuracy on a single dataset, whereas general computer-vision
research separately advances efficient backbones, imbalanced learning, attention, corruption
robustness, explainable AI, instance segmentation, and mobile deployment. A defensible state-of-
the-art review must therefore connect these directions without treating metrics from different datasets
as directly rankable. Reported results are meaningful only together with the dataset, class definitions,
preprocessing, split policy, checkpoint selection, and evaluation metric used by the original study.
This chapter follows a chronological and analytical structure. It first traces the transition from
biological disease management to image-based screening, then examines the theories and systems
that support lightweight classification and mask-level localization. The comparison tables preserve
results and claims from the reviewed publications while explicitly identifying limitations relative to
the thesis. Project-specific proposed modules and experimental results are not presented as existing
methods; they are introduced only in the later Methodology and Results chapters.
2. Historical Context
2.1 Biological and Conventional Diagnostic Foundations
Before deep learning became relevant, shrimp disease research concentrated on pathogen
emergence, transmission, clinical signs, histopathology, molecular diagnosis, and farm-level control.
Studies from the late 1990s onward documented the destructive effect of viral diseases on penaeid
shrimp and emphasized exclusion, pathogen-free stocks, movement control, and laboratory testing
[1], [2]. Later reviews connected recurrent disease emergence with the rapid globalization and
intensification of shrimp production [3], [4].
By 2012, the literature had shifted from describing individual outbreaks toward a system-level
1
37\n\n--- PAGE 46 ---\nview of food security and pathogen dissemination. Stentiford et al. argued that disease could
constrain future crustacean supply, while Flegel and Seibert and Pinto reviewed the emergence,
distribution, and biology of major shrimp pathogens [5–7]. Thitamadee et al. subsequently
summarized important disease threats facing cultivated penaeid shrimp in Asia, including syndromes
whose visible signs can overlap with environmental stress or secondary infection [8]. This history
explains why an image model should be framed as preliminary screening rather than autonomous
diagnosis.
2.2 Early Image Analysis and Transfer Learning
The first practical transition toward data-driven image recognition occurred when convolutional
neural networks became transferable to small domain datasets. Duong-Trung et al. demonstrated
that pretrained CNNs could classify multiple shrimp disease categories, establishing an early
field-image baseline for this topic [11]. The study was important because it reduced dependence
on handcrafted visual descriptors, but it remained centered on image-level prediction and did not
provide disease-region masks, mobile deployment evidence, or specimen-grouped leakage analysis.
A second stage emphasized compact custom architectures. Wang et al. modified LeNet
for rapid classification of hepatopancreatic necrosis, red-body, and whitish-muscle disease in
Penaeus vannamei [12]. This work illustrated that shrimp-specific simplification could reduce
computational cost, although its disease space and acquisition protocol differ from four-class
Healthy/BG/WSSV/co-infection screening. In parallel, Querol et al. investigated MobileNetV3-
Small and EfficientNetV2-B0 for WSSV monitoring, combined on-device considerations with
saliency heatmaps, and explicitly discussed class imbalance [13]. The progression was therefore
not merely from one architecture to another; it was a movement from offline classification toward
resource-aware and interpretable screening.
2.3 Public Datasets and Data-Centric Development
The field expanded more rapidly after the release of reusable image resources. Shrim-
pDiseaseBD and its associated ShrimpDiseaseImageBD record introduced a four-class collection
containing Healthy, BG, WSSV , and combined BG/WSSV images collected with farm collaboration
and expert supervision [14], [15]. TigerShrimpBD provided an additional public source that was later
used together with ShrimpDiseaseImageBD by a lightweight classification study [16], [17]. These
releases made independent benchmarking more feasible, but image-level labels did not remove the
need to audit repeated specimens, near duplicates, acquisition bias, or annotation uncertainty.
Adjacent aquatic-disease datasets are useful only when their domain difference is explicit.
SalmonScan, for example, supports fish-disease image research but differs in species, symptom
morphology, acquisition conditions, and class semantics [18]. Such datasets can demonstrate
broader trends in aquatic computer vision, but they should not be treated as direct substitutes for
shrimp-specific validation.
2.4 Lightweight, Explainable, and Deployable Systems
From 2024 onward, shrimp disease research increasingly combined classification with
application-oriented requirements. SHRIMPAI implemented a mobile WSSV screening workflow
2
38\n\n--- PAGE 47 ---\n[19], while Xu et al. and Fei et al. explored YOLO-based shrimp disease localization or
detection [20], [21]. Ramachandran et al. combined an enhanced gated recurrent unit with
wild-geese-migration optimization for WSSV-oriented shrimp classification [22]. These studies
broadened the field from generic transfer learning toward disease-specific deployment and
localization, although object detection remained different from instance segmentation because
bounding boxes do not describe the exact disease-region boundary.
The 2025–2026 literature further diversified. Büyükarıkan evaluated multi-model ensembles;
Aung et al. compared MobileNet and EfficientNet in a pathogen-oriented imaging setting; Raj
et al. introduced a recurrent capsule architecture; and other studies combined classification with
recommendations, IoT monitoring, or severity assessment [23–28]. ShrimpXNet is the closest
classification reference for the present thesis because it combines background removal, transfer
learning, MixUp, CutMix, adversarial training, and CAM-based explanation [29]. FeatherNetX
represents a different direction: a compact architecture evaluated through five-fold cross-validation
and two public shrimp image sources [17]. Recent MobileNetV3–KNN and explainable ensemble
studies continue this trend toward compact or interpretable systems, while additional conference
work has continued the direct shrimp-disease classification line [30–32].
3. Key Studies and Theories
3.1 Shrimp Disease Classification
Shrimp disease classification studies differ substantially in label space. Some use six disease
categories, some focus only on WSSV , and others classify pathological tissue or a small set of visibly
distinct syndromes. Duong-Trung et al. reported an early multi-disease transfer-learning result,
whereas Wang et al. designed a compact LeNet derivative for three Penaeus vannamei diseases [11],
[12]. WSSV-focused systems subsequently evaluated mobile backbones or application workflows
[13], [19], [22]. These studies demonstrate feasibility, but their numerical results cannot be placed
in a single ranking because the datasets and target definitions are not equivalent.
The more recent four-class literature is more directly relevant. ShrimpXNet evaluates several
pretrained backbones on the public four-class dataset and incorporates regularization, adversarial
training, and explanation methods [29]. FeatherNetX combines a compact backbone with public
shrimp datasets and reports model size and computational complexity in addition to classification
performance [17]. Büyükarıkan, Raj et al., and the explainable ensemble study demonstrate
alternative ensemble, capsule, and multi-backbone directions [23], [25], [31]. Together, these papers
show that the field has moved beyond asking whether CNNs can recognize shrimp disease; current
questions concern efficiency, robustness, class imbalance, interpretability, external evaluation, and
reproducibility.
ShrimpXNet must nevertheless be described carefully. It is a literature baseline and methodolog-
ical reference for classification, not the architecture directly modified by the proposed classifier in
this thesis. Its lack of a complete official reproduction package also means that any local reimple-
mentation must document data protocol, preprocessing, augmentation, architecture, optimization,
checkpoint selection, evaluation definitions, framework versions, and nondeterministic behavior
rather than attributing differences only to a generic “different environment.”
3
39\n\n--- PAGE 48 ---\n3.2 Public Shrimp and Aquatic-Disease Datasets
Dataset papers make the experimental object explicit and therefore deserve separate analysis
from model papers. ShrimpDiseaseBD documents the acquisition and class organization of the
Bangladesh shrimp disease images, while the Mendeley record preserves the downloadable dataset
version and DOI [14], [15]. TigerShrimpBD is relevant because it was used in later peer-reviewed
lightweight classification work [16], [17]. The main limitation of these sources for instance
segmentation is that they provide image-level disease categories rather than independently reviewed
disease-region masks.
A dataset description is not sufficient evidence of split validity. Multiple views of the same
physical specimen can produce highly similar images with consistent disease labels. If such
views cross training and test partitions, the model may exploit specimen-specific appearance rather
than disease-general evidence. Dataset comparison must therefore include label type, annotation
provenance, source identity, repeated-subject risk, and whether the original publication reports a
grouped or source-aware split.
3.3 Instance-Segmentation Foundations
Dense prediction developed through several architectural stages. Fully convolutional networks
and U-Net established pixel-wise semantic prediction and encoder–decoder recovery of spatial detail
[33], [34]. Mask R-CNN then extended two-stage detection with a parallel mask branch, providing
a foundational framework for instance-level masks [35]. YOLACT separated prototype-mask
generation from instance-specific coefficients, showing that real-time instance segmentation could
be obtained with a one-stage formulation [36].
Later systems targeted boundary quality, ROI-free prediction, dynamic masks, and real-time
query processing. PointRend treated segmentation as iterative rendering of uncertain points;
CondInst generated instance-specific dynamic mask heads without ROI operations; SOLOv2
represented instances by location and dynamic kernels; and Mask2Former unified several segmen-
tation tasks through masked attention [37–40]. YOLACTEdge, FastInst, and MobileInst moved
the efficiency discussion closer to edge or mobile constraints [41–43]. These methods are not
shrimp-specific, but they establish the design space for disease-region masks and clarify why
classification accuracy alone cannot validate localization quality.
3.4 Lightweight Classification Architectures
The classification baseline families benchmarked by this project originate from a long sequence
of architectural improvements. AlexNet, VGG, GoogLeNet, ResNet, DenseNet, and Xception es-
tablished progressively deeper, multi-branch, residual, densely connected, and depthwise-separable
representations [44–49]. SqueezeNet and MobileNet then made parameter count and deployment
cost explicit design objectives [50], [51].
Mobile-oriented research subsequently introduced channel shuffling, inverted residuals,
platform-aware search, hardware-aware activations, compound scaling, cheap feature generation,
reparameterization, and hybrid CNN–transformer designs [52–65]. These papers provide the
original architectural context for the TIMM screening table. Their relevance is not that every model
4
40\n\n--- PAGE 49 ---\nis suitable for the final application, but that they allow the thesis to compare accuracy and Macro-F1
against parameter count, model size, latency, and conversion feasibility.
3.5 Imbalance-Aware Learning
Disease datasets are often imbalanced because Healthy images and common disease classes may
be easier to collect than mixed or less frequent conditions. Focal Loss reduces the influence of easy
examples; Class-Balanced Loss reweights classes using the effective number of samples; LDAM
introduces class-dependent margins; and Asymmetric Loss applies different focusing behavior to
positive and negative examples [66–69]. These are existing published methods and can be compared
theoretically. The project-specific ASL–LDAM formulation, however, is not an existing related-
work method and is therefore introduced only in the Methodology chapter.
The literature also warns against assuming that one imbalance method is universally superior.
Loss behavior depends on whether the task is multiclass or multilabel, the severity of imbalance,
label noise, calibration, sampling strategy, and model capacity. A fair thesis comparison therefore
keeps the split, backbone, training schedule, augmentation, checkpoint selection, and metric
definitions controlled when evaluating an imbalance-aware objective.
3.6 Attention and Feature Recalibration
Attention modules seek to redistribute representational capacity toward informative channels or
spatial regions. Squeeze-and-Excitation models channel dependencies; CBAM applies sequential
channel and spatial attention; ECA reduces channel-attention overhead; Coordinate Attention
preserves positional information; SimAM derives parameter-free neuron importance; and Triplet
Attention models cross-dimensional interactions [70–75]. These methods provide published
foundations for controlled attention experiments in segmentation.
The review does not classify the project-specific attention configuration or DCFR module as an
existing system. DCFR is a custom, independent feature-recalibration component, and its design
and evidence belong to the proposed methodology. Likewise, an attention configuration selected
through the project’s instance segmentation experiments is a proposed configuration rather than a
peer-reviewed baseline. Related work should explain the adopted foundations without assigning
publication status to the project’s own design.
3.7 Fourier-Domain Processing for Segmentation
Frequency-domain research provides a theoretical basis for examining image degradation and
texture-sensitive segmentation. Yin et al. showed that robustness trade-offs can be interpreted
through the frequency concentration of corruptions and augmentation effects [76]. Fourier Domain
Adaptation transferred low-frequency amplitude information between source and target images for
semantic segmentation, while a later Fourier framework investigated domain generalization through
frequency manipulation [77], [78].
These studies motivate three segmentation-specific questions: whether Fourier transformations
improve robustness under task-appropriate degradation; whether they preserve or enhance evidence
for small disease regions; and whether they produce measurable benefits for instance segmentation.
Existing literature does not answer these questions for BG/WSSV masks. The project’s Fourier

listed here as published existing methods.
3.8 Robustness and Explainable AI
Robustness research distinguishes clean-test performance from stability under distribution
shift. FGSM established a simple adversarial perturbation mechanism; mixup and CutMix
regularized training through sample interpolation or region replacement; ImageNet-C formalized
common-corruption benchmarking; and AugMix improved corruption robustness through diverse
augmentation chains [79–83]. These methods support a controlled robustness protocol but do not
justify claiming resilience to every farm condition.
Explainability research progressed from class activation mapping to gradient- and score-based
variants. CAM links global-average-pooled features to class scores; Grad-CAM and Grad-
CAM++ produce class-discriminative heatmaps; Score-CAM weights activation maps using forward
scores; and Eigen-CAM produces a class-independent principal-component visualization [84–88].
Heatmaps can identify influential regions and expose obvious background dependence, but they do
not establish causality, clinical validity, or pixel-accurate disease boundaries. This distinction is
important when comparing XAI with instance segmentation.
3.9 Leakage-Aware and Reproducible Evaluation
Data leakage occurs when training or model-selection procedures gain information that would
not be available in deployment. Kaufman et al. formalized leakage as an evaluation-design
problem rather than merely a coding error [89]. In medical imaging, Zech et al. showed that
site-specific confounding can weaken cross-domain generalization; Roberts et al. documented
recurring methodological pitfalls; and Tampu et al. quantified inflated test accuracy caused by
leakage [90–92].
The analogous shrimp-dataset risk is repeated-specimen leakage. If several photographs of
the same shrimp appear in different partitions, an image-level random split can overestimate
generalization. A specimen-grouped split reduces this specific risk by keeping all known views of
one specimen together. It does not eliminate label error, acquisition-site bias, temporal confounding,
or undocumented duplicates. Reproducible evaluation therefore requires split manifests, stable
class ordering, identifiable checkpoints, recorded seeds, saved configurations, and traceable metric
scripts.
3.10 Mobile and Edge Deployment
Mobile deployment requires more than a low parameter count. Model operators must be
supported by the target runtime, preprocessing must match training, tensor order and class mapping
must remain stable, and latency must be measured on representative hardware. MobileNet and later
mobile architectures make hardware efficiency an explicit design objective [51], [53], [55], [65].
Shrimp-specific edge and mobile studies demonstrate the practical value of on-device or near-device
screening [13], [19], [27].
The recent YOLO lineage also matters because unified implementations can support classi-
fication and instance segmentation within one tooling ecosystem. The original YOLO paper
6
42\n\n--- PAGE 51 ---\nestablished single-stage real-time detection, YOLOv10 advanced end-to-end NMS-free detection,
and the YOLO26 technical paper describes a unified family covering classification and instance
segmentation [93–95]. Object-detection results are not treated as a research outcome of this thesis;
the relevance is the shared deployment-oriented framework and the availability of lightweight
classification and segmentation variants.
4. Technological Advancements
4.1 From Manual Inspection to Image-Based Screening
The earliest stage relied on visual observation supported by microscopy, histopathology, and
molecular tests. Image-based screening does not remove the need for those procedures, but it can
prioritize suspicious cases and shorten the time between observation and expert follow-up. The
responsible technological shift is therefore from unaided observation to decision support, not from
laboratory diagnosis to autonomous clinical judgment.
4.2 From Handcrafted Features to Transfer Learning
Transferred CNNs reduced the need to design disease-specific color, texture, and shape
descriptors manually. Early shrimp studies demonstrated feasibility with pretrained networks,
while later work used compact custom models, ensembles, capsule networks, and hybrid pipelines
[11], [12], [23], [25]. The principal advancement was reusable representation learning, but model
comparison remained sensitive to dataset construction and split policy.
4.3 From Heavy CNNs to Lightweight Architectures
The efficiency literature replaced raw depth with structured operations such as depthwise
convolution, channel shuffle, inverted residuals, cheap feature generation, neural architecture search,
and structural reparameterization [52–55], [57], [63]. This progression supports multi-objective
selection using Macro-F1, parameters, size, latency, and export feasibility. A heavier model is not
automatically preferable when its performance gain is small or absent.
4.4 From Image-Level Labels to Instance-Level Evidence
Classification answers which class best represents an image, whereas instance segmentation
asks where each disease region is located. The technological path from FCN and U-Net to Mask
R-CNN, YOLACT, CondInst, Mask2Former, and mobile-oriented systems gradually improved
instance separation, boundary representation, speed, and deployment feasibility [33–36], [38], [40],
[43]. For shrimp images, the principal barrier is not only architecture selection but the absence of
expert-reviewed disease-region masks in the public classification dataset.
4.5 From Clean Accuracy to Robust and Transparent Evaluation
Recent systems increasingly supplement accuracy with class-balanced metrics, corruption tests,
adversarial evaluation, and explanation maps. This shift is important because a classifier can
achieve high clean accuracy while relying on acquisition artifacts or majority-class shortcuts.
Robustness and XAI provide complementary evidence: robustness measures stability under
controlled transformations, whereas heatmaps help inspect influential image regions. Neither
7
43\n\n--- PAGE 52 ---\nreplaces external validation or mask-level localization.
4.6 From Random Splits to Leakage-Aware Protocols
Random image splitting is convenient but scientifically weak when images are clustered by
specimen, source, or acquisition session. The evaluation literature has progressively emphasized
subject-level separation, site-aware validation, and transparent reporting [89–92]. In a shrimp
dataset with repeated photographs, grouped-specimen splitting is the appropriate direct response,
while source-wise splitting before multi-dataset merging protects partition integrity.
4.7 From Offline Models to Integrated Mobile Systems
The final technological shift joins research evidence with application delivery. SHRIMPAI, the
edge-WSSV study, and the integrated IoT system show that disease models can be embedded in
mobile or edge workflows [13], [19], [27]. A complete implementation must preserve preprocessing,

6. Gaps in the Literature/Technology
The first gap is the separation between image-level classification and disease-region localization.
Existing shrimp studies predominantly predict a whole-image label or a bounding box. They rarely
provide instance masks for BG and WSSV regions, and public datasets do not include independently
reviewed mask annotations. As a result, classification confidence can be high without demonstrating
that the model has localized visible disease evidence.
The second gap concerns evaluation validity. Most shrimp image papers report image-level
splits without a detailed audit of repeated specimens, near duplicates, source overlap, or acquisition
15
51\n\n--- PAGE 60 ---\nclusters. This omission is serious because several photographs can depict the same physical
shrimp. A random split may therefore measure recognition of specimen-specific appearance rather
than generalization to unseen shrimp. The broader leakage literature shows that subject and site
confounding can substantially inflate test performance [89–92].
The third gap is incomplete robustness evidence. Some systems use augmentation or adversarial
training, but few evaluate a controlled set of task-appropriate corruptions and report class-balanced
performance. The absence of such tests is especially important for mobile farm images, where
illumination, focus, compression, background complexity, and camera characteristics can differ from
the training set. Corruption results should still be bounded carefully because no finite benchmark
can represent every farm and acquisition condition.
The fourth gap is weak comparability across studies. Reported metrics frequently use different
disease spaces, datasets, image modalities, split policies, and averaging rules. Accuracy is often
presented without Macro-F1 or classwise evidence, which can conceal poor minority-class behavior.
Model size, latency, and deployment feasibility are also reported inconsistently. A defensible project
must therefore establish controlled internal baselines rather than select a method from headline
accuracy alone.
The fifth gap concerns reproducibility and artifact traceability. Several recent systems describe
complex preprocessing, augmentation, ensembles, or optimization pipelines without releasing a
complete executable package. When code, exact splits, class order, checkpoint-selection rules, and
environment versions are unavailable, a reimplementation can legitimately differ from the paper
result. Such differences must be analyzed through data protocol, preprocessing, augmentation,
architecture, training, model selection, evaluation, and environment factors rather than attributed
to one vague cause.
The sixth gap is the limited integration of research and application evidence. Mobile studies
demonstrate useful interfaces but usually focus on binary WSSV screening, while multi-class
studies often remain offline. Instance segmentation, robustness, XAI, Firebase-backed application
workflows, and source-aware external evaluation are generally developed in separate publications.
No reviewed system reports all of these components under a single traceable project protocol.
The seventh gap is annotation quality for disease-region segmentation. Producing masks requires
both identifying the disease category and deciding where visible evidence begins and ends. This is
substantially harder than assigning a whole-image classification label. Background removal may
help classification by suppressing irrelevant context, but the same preprocessing can remove spatial
or boundary information needed for segmentation. Single-annotator labeling improves internal
consistency but leaves uncertainty because inter-annotator agreement and expert mask review are
unavailable.
7. Justification for the Project
The literature justifies a project centered on lightweight four-class classification and BG/WSSV
instance segmentation rather than object detection. Classification provides an efficient preliminary
screening mechanism for Healthy, BG, WSSV , and co-infection images. Instance segmentation
supplies a stricter, spatially explicit task that cannot be replaced by classification confidence or a
16
52\n\n--- PAGE 61 ---\nbounding box. The two tasks therefore address different evidence requirements within the same
application domain.
The classification direction is justified by the trade-off exposed in existing systems. High-
capacity models and ensembles can report strong results but may be unsuitable for mobile inference,
while compact systems can sacrifice class-balanced performance. Benchmarking representative
TIMM and official YOLO classification families under one controlled seed and split provides an
engineering basis for selecting a lightweight backbone. ShrimpXNet remains the closest published
methodological reference, but the proposed classifier is evaluated as a direct improvement to the
selected YOLO26m-cls engineering baseline rather than as a modification of ShrimpXNet.
The segmentation direction is justified by the scarcity of shrimp disease masks and the limitations
of image-level explanation. Published attention and instance segmentation methods provide
foundations, but the project-specific attention configuration and Fourier experiments are evaluated
as proposed research. The Fourier study directly tests whether frequency-domain transformations
improve robustness, small-region evidence, or instance segmentation performance across multiple
configurations. Its conclusions must be based on the recorded experiments rather than on theoretical
expectation alone.
Leakage-aware evaluation is justified by repeated-specimen risk. Grouping known views of the
same shrimp protects the segmentation test set from one important source of information overlap.
Source-wise splitting before multi-dataset merging similarly prevents images from being moved
across partitions after combination. These measures do not guarantee universal generalization, but
they provide a more credible estimate than an undocumented image-level random split.
Finally, mobile integration is justified as an application layer after the research methods are
evaluated. TensorFlow Lite conversion, Android CPU inference, result and mask display, history
management, Firebase services, and administrator functions demonstrate that selected models can
participate in a usable workflow. The application remains a preliminary screening prototype: it does
not replace laboratory diagnosis, veterinary assessment, treatment prescription, or formal farmer
user-acceptance testing.
Taken together, the project is necessary because the reviewed literature rarely combines
lightweight classification, class-imbalance handling, robustness testing, XAI, mask-level disease
localization, leakage-aware splitting, multi-source evaluation, reproducibility artifacts, and mobile
deployment. The intended contribution is not to claim superiority over incomparable published per-
centages. It is to construct and evaluate a traceable research pipeline in which each methodological
claim is tied to a controlled baseline, a documented dataset protocol, and explicit limitations.

1. Methodological Framework and Experimental Scope
This study was organized around two complementary computer-vision tasks. The first task was
four-class image classification, in which each input image was assigned to Healthy, Black Gill dis-
ease (BG), White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV), or the WSSV_BG co-infection category. The sec-
ond task was disease-region instance segmentation, in which visible BG and WSSV regions were
represented by instance masks. Classification provided a compact image-level decision suitable for
mobile-oriented screening, whereas instance segmentation provided spatial evidence for inspecting
localized symptoms and evaluation leakage.
Three controlled experimental branches were maintained. The classification branch compared can-
didate backbones under a fixed seed-42 image partition, then trained a YOLO26m-cls classifier with
the proposed SimAM-DCFR feature recalibration and the training-only ASL-LDAM objective. The
attention-based segmentation branch inserted feature-recalibration modules immediately before the
YOLO11n-seg Segment head. The Fourier branch applied image-space frequency transformations,
and separately considered feature-space frequency refinement, without conflating either branch with
the classification method. Model conversion and runtime measurements were treated as downstream
feasibility checks; mobile interface behavior is described in Chapter V rather than in this chapter.
The methodology separated training operations from inference operations. Background removal,
augmentation, feature recalibration, custom losses, checkpoint selection, corruption generation, and
explanation-map generation were recorded as distinct stages. Performance outcomes, model rankings,
confusion matrices, corruption gains, and comparative conclusions are reserved for Chapter VI.
2. Classification Methodology
2.1 Classification Image Preparation and Fixed Experimental Protocol
The classification source contained four image-level categories. Each file was loaded as an RGB
image, processed through an offline foreground-extraction stage, and written to a standardized direc-
tory tree while preserving filename identity. The background-removal stage was completed before
model training so that directly compared classifiers received the same stored image representation. A
class-order check enforced the serialized order Healthy, BG, WSSV , and WSSV_BG in manifests,
model outputs, metric scripts, and deployment metadata.
A fixed seed-42 manifest divided the background-removed source into training, validation, and test
partitions. All classification candidates used the same partition membership. Training samples were
resized or cropped to 224 × 224, converted to tensors, normalized, and augmented using the retained
Ultralytics classification configuration with RandAugment enabled. V alidation and test samples used
deterministic resize and normalization without stochastic augmentation. The procedural controls are
summarized in Table 4.1.

3. Instance-Segmentation Methodology
3.1 Disease-Region Annotation Protocol and Label Semantics
The official ShrimpDiseaseImageBD source provides image-level disease categories but does not provide
instance-segmentation polygons or masks. The project team therefore created the disease-region annotations
used in this branch. The segmentation label space contained two positive mask classes: BG and WSSV .
WSSV_BG remained an image-level classification category and was not introduced as a third mask class. In
co-infection images, spatially separable BG and WSSV evidence could be annotated as separate instances.
BG polygons were assigned to visibly darkened gill regions, whereas WSSV polygons were assigned to
visible white-spot or white-lesion regions. Shadows, reflections, background marks, and weak texture changes
were excluded when they were not visually consistent with the target symptom. Healthy was never defined as
a segmentation class and received no positive disease-mask supervision.
All masks were produced by the project team rather than by an aquaculture pathologist. No independent
expert boundary review or inter-annotator agreement study was available. The masks therefore represent a
consistent project annotation protocol, not clinical ground truth, and this is a material limitation of the study.

Gi = Diseasei :: ShrimpID i. (4.21)

Gi = Gj ⇒ Si = Sj, (4.22)

7. Methodological Validity and Scope Boundaries
The evidence was generated primarily under fixed-seed protocols and therefore does not estab-
lish multi-seed statistical significance. Classification candidate screening inspected the recorded test-
partition Macro-F1, which limits the interpretation of that partition as an untouched model-selection-
independent test set. Segmentation masks were produced under a single-annotator protocol without
independent expert boundary review, and specimen identity was derived from filenames rather than
a farm-side identity registry. The attention configurations were not executed under fully identical
augmentation, epoch, early-stopping, and software-version conditions; comparisons must therefore
remain tied to their recorded protocols.
Controlled synthetic corruptions represented selected degradations and did not constitute field val-
idation. XAI maps were qualitative explanations of model activations, not causal or clinical evidence.
Fourier preprocessing introduced a separate runtime stage that must be timed independently from
model inference. Finally, the methods were designed for preliminary image-based screening and
research evaluation; they do not replace laboratory testing, veterinary assessment, or clinical decision-
making.

The chapter describes how the image-classification and disease-region instance-segmentation components
were connected to a native Android application. The emphasis is the implemented control flow, the model–
application interface, deployment packaging, persistent records, and maintenance boundaries. Mathematical
definitions, model-training procedures, and evaluation metrics are specified in Part IV; numerical model out-
comes are reserved for Part VI. The application is treated as a preliminary screening prototype rather than a
veterinary or laboratory diagnostic system.
1. AI Model Integration
1.1 Overall System Architecture
The system was organized as a layered mobile pipeline rather than as a set of independent screens. The
presentation layer was implemented as a native Android interface, with CameraX and gallery selection serving
as image-entry mechanisms. Kotlin, Jetpack Compose, dependency injection through Hilt, and a view-model-
based orchestration layer were identified in the implementation diagram as the principal application components.
The inference path was separated from the user-interface code so that image acquisition, preprocessing, model
execution, output decoding, result presentation, and persistence could be inspected and modified independently.
At the beginning of a screening session, the user either captured an image or selected an existing image. The
selected bitmap was not passed directly to the classifier. It first entered an image-preprocessing component that
resized the image, performed the configured foreground preparation, normalized RGB values, and constructed
the model tensor. The configured classification artifact was then executed through a TensorFlow Lite/LiteRT
interpreter. Output decoding produced the class scores, the highest-ranked class, a confidence value, and the
three highest-ranked predictions used by the result interface.
A disease-decision stage controlled the transition from image-level screening to spatial evidence. Healthy
was not a positive mask class, but original Healthy images had already been included in segmentation training
as empty-label negatives containing zero BG or WSSV instances. The gate was therefore retained as an addi-
tional risk-control layer because residual false-positive disease masks still occurred on some Healthy evaluation
images. A Healthy decision, or an output below the configured application threshold, does not dispatch segmen-
tation; a disease-directed decision routes the validated image to the BG/WSSV decoder. This policy can reduce
unnecessary masks on Healthy inputs, but a classifier false negative may suppress localization for a diseased
image, so the complete cascade requires end-to-end sensitivity and error-propagation evaluation.
The result object contained the checked image, predicted class, confidence, ranked alternatives, mask data
when generated, and runtime fields. A user could then save the result to history. The architecture specifies
that results were not saved automatically after inference; the save action passed through a prediction-log repos-
itory, with duplicate saves in the same session prevented. The persistence boundary included local storage
and a Firebase/Firestore-backed repository when configured. Administrator-side functions reused the inference
pipeline and added farmer management, prediction-log review, model settings, and data-review functions with-

1.2 Classification Model Integration
The mobile classification path was configured for the YOLO26m-cls model trained with ASL-

predicted class from the four model scores. This separation preserves the distinction established in
Part IV: ASL-LDAM determines how the model is optimized, whereas inference uses the exported
network output and a deterministic class-index mapping.

1.3 Instance-Segmentation Integration
The segmentation component provides spatial evidence for BG and WSSV predictions, and its positive
output space is restricted to those two mask classes. Healthy is not represented by a segmentation class identifier;
however, original Healthy images were retained during optimization as empty-label negatives containing no BG
or WSSV polygons. WSSV_BG remains an image-level co-infection category rather than a third mask label.
For a co-infection decision, the segmentation output may contain BG regions, WSSV regions, or both, subject
to the selected checkpoint and operating thresholds.
The architecture diagram specifies a YOLO11n-seg TensorFlow Lite artifact and a decoder that receives
detection output and prototype-mask output. The postprocessing sequence filters predicted mask instances by
BG and WSSV class, applies non-maximum suppression, reconstructs the corresponding mask, and overlays
the result on the checked image. The overlay convention assigns blue to BG and purple to WSSV . These colours
are presentation metadata; the model contract is the numeric class index and mask tensor, not the screen colour.
The integrated decision rule is conservative. When the image-level classifier returns Healthy, or when the
output is below the application threshold, the result object contains no disease mask. For

receives detections but reconstructs an empty mask, the architecture includes a fallback box-derived
region. Such a fallback must be distinguishable from a decoded polygonal mask because the two
carry different spatial precision. The interface should not present either representation as clinical
proof; both are model-generated attention regions intended to support preliminary review.
The source screenshots demonstrate that the result interface can display a disease-region
overlay and ranked class probabilities. They do not, by themselves, establish that every displayed

implemented evidence supports the mask-display path and the designed LiteRT decoder, while
full end-to-end device verification of the selected instance segmentation checkpoint remains a
deployment-hardening requirement. This distinction prevents the mobile interface demonstration
from being interpreted as a completed segmentation benchmark.

2. Data Flow and Processing
2.1 Image Acquisition and Validation
The screening workflow begins from two explicit user actions: capture a new image or select
an existing image. The home screen provides both entry points, while the capture/upload screen
isolates the imageselection task from inference. This design prevents an unintended camera frame
or gallery preview from being treated as an accepted model input. The architecture further records
that inference begins only after the user confirms the selected image.
The acquisition layer receives a camera result or gallery URI and resolves it into an application-
accessible bitmap. V alidation occurs before model preprocessing. At minimum, the application
must confirm that the image can be decoded, that the resulting dimensions are non-zero, and that
the bitmap can be converted into the expected RGB representation. Orientation metadata must be
applied consistently when the Android decoder exposes it; otherwise, the visual orientation shown to
the user can differ from the orientation evaluated by the model. The available screenshots confirm
the user flow and image-quality guidance but do not expose every permission or EXIF-handling
branch, so those implementation details are not claimed beyond the verified screen behavior.
The capture interface includes guidance to place the shrimp within the frame, use adequate
lighting, avoid strong blur, and keep the device stable. These instructions do not change the
model but reduce preventable acquisition failures. They are especially relevant because mobile
preprocessing cannot recover disease evidence that is absent due to severe blur, clipping, or
occlusion. Image-quality guidance is therefore a front-end control that complements, rather than
replaces, robustness evaluation.

2.2 Preprocessing and Inference Orchestration
The ordered classification path was implemented as user image → validation → preprocessing
→ tensor creation → LiteRT inference→ output decoding. The orchestration layer coordinates these
operations but does not embed them in a single UI callback. This modularity makes it possible to
6
79\n\n--- PAGE 88 ---\ntest image preprocessing independently from score decoding and to distinguish model latency from
image preparation time.
For the configured classifier, preprocessing resized the accepted RGB image to 224 × 224.
The system architecture further records U2Net background removal, an edge-based fallback, RGB
normalization, and tensor construction. The fixed input metadata shown by the application is
[1,224,224,3], so the active classifier path writes an NHWC tensor. All channel and scaling oper-
ations must be deterministic at inference. Training-only augmentation, corruption transformations,
ASL-LDAM margin construction, and label smoothing are deliberately absent from this path.
The interpreter stage initializes the model, allocates tensors, writes the prepared input, executes
the network, and reads the four output values. Postprocessing converts the output into the probability
representation expected by the UI, validates that the values are finite, and constructs a ranked list. If
the exported model already returns normalized probabilities, a second softmax would be incorrect;
if it returns logits, probability conversion is required. The deployment sanity test therefore checks
both the tensor metadata and the behavior on a known image rather than relying on a filename alone.
The segmentation path was designed as validated image → segmentation preprocessing →
selected instance segmentation checkpoint → detection/prototype decoding → class filtering and
NMS → mask reconstruction → overlay generation. Classification and segmentation may use
different input resolutions and normalization rules. The Android orchestration layer must not reuse
the classification tensor as the segmentation tensor unless the segmentation export contract explicitly
matches it.
The disease decision lies between the two interpreters. This placement avoids unnecessary
segmentation execution for Healthy or low-confidence outputs and enforces the no-fabricated-mask
rule. It also means that a segmentation failure does not erase a valid classification result. The
result screen can present the image-level prediction with a clear message that spatial evidence was
unavailable, rather than substituting an arbitrary region.
2.3 Result Interpretation and Presentation
The result interface converts model outputs into a bounded screening report. The primary
card presents the predicted disease label and confidence. A supporting section presents the ranked
alternatives, enabling the user to distinguish a concentrated output from a close competition among
disease categories. A disease explanation describes the visible condition in non-prescriptive
language. The interface also provides actions for saving the record, checking another image,
returning to the home screen, or reviewing history.
For diseased cases, the result interface includes the segmentation overlay when available. The
mask is presented on the source image rather than as an isolated tensor because spatial evidence is
meaningful only in relation to shrimp anatomy. The associated text identifies it as a model-estimated
region requiring attention. It must not be described as histopathological confirmation, causal
explanation, or a guaranteed boundary. A model can focus on a visually correlated region and still
be wrong about the disease class or the mask extent.

2.4 History, Export, Deletion, and Feedback Flow
History is a user-controlled persistence stage rather than an automatic side effect of inference.
After reviewing a result, the user may save a prediction record. The architecture routes that action
through a prediction-log repository and prevents duplicate saves within the same session. This
design avoids populating history with abandoned or repeated checks and gives the user an explicit
decision over whether an image and its output are retained.
A history entry may contain the image thumbnail or reference, predicted label, confidence,
timestamp, processing time, FPS, and the model identifier associated with the prediction. Retaining
the model identifier is essential because the same image can produce different outputs after a model
update. Without model-version metadata, later feedback cannot be attributed to the artifact that
generated the result.

3. Deployment Strategy
3.1 Model Conversion and Packaging
Deployment began from a frozen research checkpoint rather than from a mutable training
directory. The selected classification network was exported to a TensorFlow Lite/LiteRT artifact and
packaged with the Android application. The configured filename, yolo26m_asl_ldam_simam_dcfr_
fp32.tflite, indicates a floating-point artifact and records the model family and proposed training
configuration. A filename alone is not sufficient verification; the exported file also had to expose
the expected input and output tensors and execute a known image without class-index inversion.
Conversion verification was therefore divided into structural and behavioral checks. Structural
checks inspect the input shape, output shape, data type, operator support, and model-file identity.
Behavioral checks run a known image through the converted artifact, compare preprocessing with
the offline pipeline, confirm that the expected class index maps to the same label, and inspect whether
confidence values are decoded correctly. An Android build that merely loads the file does not
establish numerical parity.
The classification artifact reports [1,224,224,3] input and [1,4] output. These definitions were
stored with class-order metadata rather than inferred from screen labels. The segmentation artifact
requires a separate contract becauseYOLO-style instance segmentation producesdetection fieldsand
prototype-mask data rather than a single class vector. Packaging must therefore include the decoder
version and mask-class mapping in addition to the model file.
Operator compatibility was evaluated conservatively. The demonstrated path used CPU
execution and XNNPACK, while the UI indicates that GPU execution was not available in the current
configuration. No claim is made for NNAPI or GPU-delegate performance. A future quantized
or delegated artifact must be treated as a separate release because changed precision and operator
placement can alter both numerical output and runtime behavior.
3.2 Android Runtime Deployment
The application was implemented as a native Android project with Kotlin as the primary
language and Java interoperability where required by dependencies or generated interfaces. Jetpack
Compose provided the declarative screen layer, while CameraX handled image capture. Hilt was
identified in the system architecture as the dependency-injection mechanism, allowing repositories,
preprocessors, and interpreters to be supplied through interfaces rather than constructed directly by
each screen.
The runtime sequence begins when the inference screen receives a validated image and the user
confirms its use. A view model coordinates background execution so that bitmap preparation and
model invocation do not block screen interaction. The interpreter is initialized with the selected
model artifact, CPU execution, and the configured thread count. Input conversion writes the model
tensor; output decoding then creates a domain-level prediction object consumed by the Compose
UI.
The application separates initialization errors from inference errors. A missing or incompatible

another image. A segmentation-decoder error preserves the classification output. A persistence
error occurs after the result has been presented and should not be reported as a model failure.

3.3 Firebase and Administrator-Side Services
Firebase was integrated as the application data layer for account-oriented and synchronized
functions. The revised application description includes authentication, profiles, prediction history,
administrator functions, and data synchronization. The architecture identifies Firebase/Firestore
at the repository boundary but does not provide an auditable database schema, collection layout,
storage-bucket path, Cloud Function, securityrule definition, or REST interface. Those details are
therefore not invented in this chapter.
The local inference path is independent from Firebase availability. Camera or gallery input,
preprocessing, classification, and result presentation execute on the device. A cloud service becomes
relevant when the user signs in, saves a synchronized record, updates a profile, or when an
administrator reviews permitted data. This boundary limits the effect of network failure: a failed
synchronization should produce an explicit storage status rather than invalidating the local model
output.
The administrator branch shown in Figure 5.1 includes a dashboard, farmer management,
administrator inference, data review, and model settings. It connects to prediction logs, farmer

implementation. It does not imply unrestricted access to user images; administrator visibility must

inference. Firebase supports application data and collaboration functions rather than replacing the
mobile interpreter. This distinction is operationally important because it defines what remains
available offline and what requires an authenticated network connection.
3.4 Deployment Procedure
The deployment procedure was defined as a controlled release workflow rather than a one-time
file copy. The steps below preserve the relationship between the research checkpoint, converted
artifact, Android configuration, and traceable release record.
1. Freeze the selected research checkpoint and record its model identifier, dataset version, split
policy, class order, input size, and preprocessing version.
2. Export the approved classifier to TensorFlow Lite/LiteRT using the intended precision,
and export the selected segmentation checkpoint only when its mobile decoder contract is
available.
3. Inspect each artifact’s tensor shapes, data types, and operators; reject an export that requires
unsupported operators or exposes unexpected dimensions.
4. Run a desktop-side sanity input through the exported artifact and verify class-index mapping
against the source checkpoint.
5. Copy the approved artifact into the Android project and update model metadata without
14
87\n\n--- PAGE 96 ---\nrenaming an unverified file as a validated release.
6. Configure input normalization, tensor layout, class labels, confidence handling, segmentation
mask mapping, and runtime threads from the release metadata.
7. Build the Android application and confirm that interpreter initialization succeeds on the target
device.
8. Run a known-image sanity test and compare the image orientation, foreground preparation,
predicted label, confidence decoding, and top-three ordering with the verified export behavior.
9. For a diseased case, verify mask decoding, class colour, overlay alignment, and the no-mask
behavior for Healthy or low-confidence output.
10. Save the result and verify history, duplicateprevention, export, deletion, localpersistence,
andFirebase synchronization in the supported configuration.
11. Record the application version, model version, deployment date, device, thread configuration,
and observed runtime diagnostics.
12. Retain the previous working model and application package so that a conversion, runtime, or
UI regression can be rolled back.
This sequence distinguishes successful conversion from successful deployment. Conversion verifies
the model artifact; deployment additionally verifies preprocessing parity, semantic decoding,
Android compatibility, screen behavior, persistence, and rollback readiness.

4. Scalability and Maintenance
4.1 Modular Design and Replaceable Components
Scalability in this prototype refers to controlled replacement and extension of components,
nottodistributed cloud inference. The architecture separates image acquisition, preprocessing,
classification, segmentation, postprocessing, presentation, persistence, and administrator functions.
Each layer exchanges a defined data object rather than accessing model tensors or Firebase state
through unrelated screens.
This separation allows the classification model to be replaced without rewriting camera capture
or history, provided the new artifact preserves the interface contract. A change in input size, class
count, class order, normalization, or output interpretation is not a drop-in replacement and requires
coordinated updates. The same rule applies to segmentation: a new checkpoint can reuse the overlay
screen only if its detection fields, prototype-mask representation, class mapping, and coordinate
conversion are compatible with the decoder.
The repository boundary similarly allows local persistence and Firebase-backed synchronization
to evolve without changing inference. The view model receives a result object and requests save,
export, or deletion through repository interfaces. Such modularity reduces coupling, but it does not
eliminate integration testing. A type-compatible component can still introduce semantic regressions,
such as a reversed class order or a changed normalization range.

4.3 Dataset and Feature Growth
New farms, cameras, acquisition conditions, external datasets, and improved masks can extend
the evidence base, but they cannot be appended without control. New data must preserve source
identity, acquisition metadata when available, and partition integrity. Images from the same physical
specimen or acquisition burst should not be scattered across training and evaluation partitions.
External sources should remain identifiable so that cross-source behavior can be assessed rather
than hidden by an undifferentiated merge.

protocol is documented.
4.4 Maintenance and Regression Verification
Maintenance is triggered by evidence, including repeated low-confidence outputs, recurrent false
positives on Healthy images, user-corrected labels, poor mask alignment, difficult lighting, blur,
background changes, conversion incompatibility, runtime slowdown, or Firebase failure. Cases
should be stored with their original prediction, model version, source image subject to consent, and
failure category. The original output must not be overwritten by the correction.
A replacement model should be compared with the deployed artifact under both research
and application criteria. Classification review includes Macro-F1, classwise precision and recall,
confusion behavior, and Healthy-versus-disease errors. Segmentation review includes the relevant
mask metrics, disease missed-image behavior, false positives on verified negative images, mask
count, and qualitative boundary inspection. Deployment review includes file size, conversion
success, operator support, preprocessing parity, device latency, memory behavior, mask-decoder
compatibility, and UI class mapping.

4.5 Security, Privacy, and Operational Boundaries
The application handles account information, shrimp images, model outputs, and history records.
Users should retain control over whether a result is saved, exported, synchronized, or deleted. The
architecture’s explicit save action supports this principle. Deletion must remove the record from
the storage destinations supported by the current configuration rather than only hiding it from the
screen.
Local inference and cloud synchronization are separate trust boundaries. The classifier can
execute without transmitting the image to a remote inference service. Firebase-dependent account
and synchronization functions require network and authentication state. When those services are
unavailable, the application should report the persistence state clearly and avoid repeated silent
retries that create duplicate records.
Administrator functions require stricter access than farmer-facing screening. The system
description confirms a basic dashboard and data-review branch but does not provide evidence
for detailed authorization rules. Therefore, the design requirement is role-restricted access to
profiles, prediction logs, consented images, and model settings; no claim is made about a specific
security-rule implementation.
The application does not prescribe medication, select treatment, replace laboratory testing, or
19
92\n\n--- PAGE 101 ---\nprovide autonomous veterinary diagnosis. Confidence is a model output, not a clinical probability.
A disease mask is a spatial model estimate, not histological evidence. Failure-safe presentation
should recommend professional or laboratory confirmation when disease is suspected and should
avoid deterministic language when the model is uncertain.
4.6 Current Limitations and Future Hardening
The demonstrated application remains a functional prototype with limited device coverage.
CPU latency depends on processor capability, thread scheduling, foreground preprocessing, model
precision, interpreter initialization, and Android runtime conditions. GPU and NNAPI delegate
behavior has not been established by the available evidence. The prototype observation shown in
history is not suitable for claiming real-time performance across devices.
The selected classification artifact has visible tensor metadata and an implemented result flow,
but conversion parity should continue to be verified with saved known-image predictions and artifact
hashes. For segmentation, the integrated design and mask-display interface are documented, yet
complete proof of the final selected checkpoint running end to end on the target device remains
incomplete. This limitation should be resolved by recording model metadata, decoder tests, mask-
alignment cases, and device-level runtime separately from classification.
External evidence remains limited by the available datasets and acquisition sources. The
segmentation masks were produced under a single-annotator protocol, and formal inter-annotator
agreement is unavailable. The application has not undergone formal farmer user-acceptance testing.
Usability, confidence interpretation, consent, offline behavior, and failure messaging therefore
require structured testing with representative users before field-readiness claims are made.
Future hardening should prioritize deterministic preprocessing tests, signed or hashed model
manifests, rollback automation, cross-device runtime measurement, segmentation-decoder unit tests,
repository synchronization tests, explicit authorization review, and regression suites covering known
failures. These measures would improve traceability and operational reliability without changing the
system’s bounded role as an image-based preliminary screening tool.

6. Classification Error Analysis
Three evidence constraints shape the error analysis. First, the primary prediction file was not
retained, so exact error transitions are unavailable. Second, the background-removal process can
suppress irrelevant context but can also remove weak edge or texture information. Third, BG, WSSV ,
and WSSV_BG share visible patterns, and a single image may contain small lesions, pose variation,
occlusion, or illumination effects that make the class boundary ambiguous.
The Combined-4 classwise report in Section 8 shows a high WSSV precision but lower WSSV
recall, together with high WSSV_BG recall and lower precision. That pattern is consistent with some
singledisease images being absorbed by the co-infection class or with mixed visual evidence being
over-predicted. It is a dataset-specific observation rather than proof that the same transition dominated
the original 173image test set.

16. Threats to Validity and Limitations
The classification evidence uses one fixed seed, so run-to-run variance and statistical significance are un-
known. Test-partition Macro-F1 was inspected during candidate screening, weakening model-family ranking
independence. Complete primary classwise predictions and a primary confusion matrix were not retained,
which limits exact error attribution. Dataset coverage remains constrained by public sources, class imbalance,
acquisition bias, and background removal; the mapped ShrimpDB-3 and Combined-4 experiments involved
retraining and are not direct cross-dataset inference.
Segmentation specimen identities were inferred from filenames rather than independently verified metadata.
One annotator produced the masks without inter-annotator agreement or expert boundary review, and determin-
istic single-label overlap handling simplified ambiguous tissue. Attention runs differed in augmentation, epoch
budget, early stopping, software version, placement, and auxiliary loss; the combined extension additionally
used a mixed grouped-stratified split. These results are comparative records, not a strict one-factor ablation.
Robustness evidence covers selected synthetic conditions only, and classification and segmentation used
different corruption families and reporting rules. The segmentation table contains only the five largest positive
gains. XAI remains qualitative, Fourier preprocessing introduces latency and false-positive trade-offs, and
mobile evidence is limited to a prototype device observation without formal farmer testing, complete delegate
evaluation, or proof of final segmentation deployment. The system

17. Recommendations
Future classification work should use validation-only model selection, retain an untouched final
test partition, and run at least three to five seeds for the CE and proposed configurations. Frozen
prediction files should support paired analysis, classwise reports, count and normalized confusion
matrices, calibration, confidence-based failure analysis, and reproducible hashes for datasets, splits,
checkpoints, environments, and conversions.
The segmentation dataset should receive independent multi-annotator review with mask-IoU or
boundary-agreement analysis. Specimen identities should be strengthened through source metadata
and duplicate detection, while external testing should preserve specimen, farm, and source groups. A
unified corruption benchmark should apply common families, severity levels, aggregation rules, and
full positive-and-negative reporting to both tasks.
Deployment evaluation should compare quantized and FP32 artifacts under one interface contract
and separate decode, preprocessing, initialization, inference, postprocessing, and UI timing. Cross-
device CPU and supported-delegate profiling should record warm-up, thread count, hashes, app ver-
sion, and device identifiers. The final segmentation checkpoint requires explicit TFLite/LiteRT export
and an end-to-end mask regression suite. A controlled user study should verify that confidence, masks,
limitations, and escalation guidance are understood without being interpreted as diagnosis.
18. Personal Reflection
The principal methodological lesson was that a large metric is not sufficient evidence. Baseline
reproduction, split integrity, artifact provenance, negative-image behavior, and protocol comparability
materially changed the interpretation of the results. The specimen-grouped analysis was particularly
important because it showed that a convenient split could overstate localization performance.
Model research and system integration remained distinct: a controlled GPU gain did not imply
mobile speed, and higher segmentation mAP could coincide with more Healthy false positives. Future
work should preserve exact protocols, expose trade-offs, and keep claims bounded.

1. Summary of Findings
1.1 Project Scope and Evidence Branches
The completed project established a connected research and implementation pipeline for shrimp-
disease image analysis. Its first evidence branch addressed four-class, single-label classification in
the fixed order Healthy, BG, WSSV , and WSSV_BG. Its second branch addressed disease-region
instance segmentation with BG and WSSV masks, while Healthy images remained empty-label neg-
atives. These branches were supplemented by controlled robustness tests, qualitative explainability,
leakage-aware evaluation, attention and Fourier investigations, and a native Android integration path.
The branches should not be collapsed into one performance claim: classification measures image-level
decisions, segmentation measures spatial localization, and mobile observations measure implementa-
tion behavior on a specific runtime configuration.
This separation was important throughout the thesis. Classification was designed for compact
preliminary screening, whereas segmentation was designed to expose spatial evidence and negative-
image failure behavior. Robustness experiments measured sensitivity to selected synthetic transforma-
tions rather than field deployment. Explainability panels provided qualitative inspection of influential
regions rather than causal or clinical localization. The Android prototype connected a frozen classi-
fication artifact to a user workflow, but application integration was treated as an engineering result
distinct from offline model evaluation.
1.2 Classification Findings
The controlled classification reference was YOLO26m-cls trained with cross-entropy. The final
selected configuration retained the same reference architecture and introduced the training-only ASL-
LDAM objective together with SimAM-DCFR feature recalibration. Under the recorded fixed seed-42
protocol, accuracy increased from 89.02% to 91.33%, Macro-F1 increased from 89.02% to 91.01%,
and Cohen’s Kappa on the displayed (×)100 scale increased from 85.05 to 88.16. The corresponding
Macro-F1 change was +1.99 percentage points. Because the comparison was based on one fixed seed,
it is an observed improvement under that protocol rather than evidence of statistical significance.
The final result must also be interpreted with the retained artifact boundary. The primary prediction-
level file, complete classwise report, and primary count and normalized confusion matrices were not
retained for the 173-image test partition. Consequently, the overall improvement cannot be decom-
posed reliably into exact class transitions. The thesis correctly avoided reconstructing those artifacts
from rounded aggregate values. This omission limits class-specific interpretation but preserves factual
integrity.
The additional-source experiments qualified the main finding. On ShrimpDB-3, the proposed con-
figuration reached 91.49% accuracy and 91.29% Macro-F1, compared with 70.21% accuracy and
72.30% Macro-F1 for the CE reference. On Combined-4, however, the CE reference reached 87.73%
accuracy and 86.51% Macro-F1, while the proposed configuration reached 83.18% accuracy and
82.18% Macro-F1. Both experiments retrained the models on their mapped datasets; they were not
direct external inference by the original checkpoint. The opposite outcomes show that the integrated
method was sensitive to source composition, class priors, and acquisition differences. No universal-
superiority claim is justified.
Across the five retained aggregate classification corruptions, the proposed classifier exceeded the
CE reference in Macro-F1 and Kappa. The Macro-F1 gains were +7.32 points for impulse noise,
+3.71 for Gaussian noise, +6.03 for contrast reduction, +5.67 for defocus blur, and +7.08 for low

1.3 Instance-Segmentation Findings
The segmentation branch showed that split design materially affected apparent localization perfor-
mance. Random image-level splitting allowed repeated views of the same filename-derived specimen
to cross partitions, producing optimistic results. The specimen-grouped protocol removed documented
train-test specimen overlap and became the principal validity-oriented evaluation. The lower grouped-
split metrics should not be interpreted as methodological failure; they represent a stricter estimate on
unseen known specimens.
Healthy-negative diagnostics added information that mask mAP alone could not provide. Healthy
was not a mask class, and the 403 Healthy images contained no positive disease-region annotation.
Measuring whether these images produced one or more retained disease masks exposed false-positive
behavior that positive-only localization metrics would hide. Disease missed-image rate and mask-
count mean absolute error similarly separated image-level safety and counting behavior from average
precision.
The attention configurations exhibited metric-specific trade-offs. CA-to-SimAM led the recorded
labeled-only localization metrics and internal HScore in the original grouped comparison, LKA-to-
SimAM led full-test mAP50 and disease missed-image rate, and DPCA minimized Healthy false posi-

augmentation, epoch budget, early stopping, initialization, software versions, placement, and auxiliary
loss differed, these rows were not a strict one-factor ablation. The evidence supports configuration-
level observations, not isolated causal attribution to one attention mechanism.
Fourier preprocessing was also condition-dependent. Under the strict no-augmentation control, W1
high-pass preprocessing with σ = 50 and α = 0.10 increased labeled mAP50 from 23.54% to 34.51%.
At the same time, the Healthy false-positive rate increased from 39.02% to 63.41%, and effective pro-
cessing time reached 87.38 ms per image in the recorded CUDA timing scope, including 48.62 ms of
Fourier preprocessing. W1 improved selected augmentation regimes and reduced performance in oth-
ers. These trade-offs rule out a general claim that frequency enhancement consistently complements
conventional augmentation.
The retained segmentation corruption table contained the five largest positive-gain conditions
rather than a complete corruption benchmark. It therefore demonstrates that selected gains existed
under color cast, turbidity, JPEG artifacts, overexposure, and Gaussian noise, but it does not establish
improvement across all tested conditions. The separate qualitative Gaussian-blur panel must not be
conflated with the quantitative Gaussian-noise row.

The overall contribution lies in the combination of research controls and engineering traceability
rather than in an unbounded model ranking. The classification branch established a controlled CE
reference, implemented a single-label imbalance-aware objective, and separated published parameter-
free SimAM from the project-created trainable texture and channel-gating paths. The evaluation then

The segmentation branch contributed specimen-grouped evaluation, explicit leakage quantifica-

under their actual protocols. The deployment branch preserved class mapping, tensor metadata, re-

research and implementation pipeline. They do not establish clinical validity, universal robustness, or
field readiness.
2. Contributions and Reflections on the Project
2.1 Technical Contributions
The classification implementation combined a custom single-label softmax ASL-LDAM objective
with YOLO26m-cls. LDAM margins were applied only to the true-class logit before softmax, while
asymmetric weights were computed for the weighted negative log-likelihood. This differs from treat-
ing ASL as a generic multilabel sigmoid loss. ASL-LDAM remained training-only and did not add
operators to the mobile inference graph.
The SimAM-DCFR integration preserved the published SimAM branch while adding project-
created texture recalibration, channel gating, and residual fusion. Only the SimAM branch was
parameter-free; the depthwise and pointwise texture convolutions and the channel gate were trainable.
This distinction is technically important because describing the full integration as parameter-free
would understate its model and runtime implications.
The project also completed classification candidate screening, attention experiments for instance
segmentation, image-space Fourier preprocessing studies, and Android classification integration.
These components expanded the evidence beyond one clean classification score. However, the
retained experiments do not prove that each individual component was independently superior. The
strongest claim is that the final integrated classifier improved the CE reference under the recorded
fixed-seed protocol and that the segmentation configurations exposed measurable trade-offs under
their documented settings.

The project defined a reproducible four-class mapping and carried the same serialized order into
the FP32 mobile artifact. The Android integration exposed input and output tensor shapes, execution
mode, thread count, model identifier, ranked outputs, and persistent history. These fields provide the
minimum traceability needed to connect a saved prediction to the artifact and runtime that produced
it.
The application workflow also separated local inference from persistence and synchronization re-
sponsibilities. A Firebase interruption does not change the locally generated model output, whileuser-
controlled save, export, and deletion actions preserve operational boundaries. The prototype demon-
strates screening-oriented interaction and debugging support; it does not demonstrate adoption, vet-
erinary validation, or field effectiveness.
2.4 Project Reflection
Several practices worked well. Iterative baseline reconstruction clarified the relationship between
the ShrimpXNet-oriented comparison protocol and the independently selected YOLO26m-cls refer-
ence. Artifact verification prevented obsolete or unsupported values from entering the final chapter.
Modular experimentation made it possible to separate classification, attention segmentation, Fourier
processing, robustness, XAI, and deployment evidence. Explicit validity analysis exposed the con-
sequences of test-side screening, repeated-specimen leakage, negative-image false positives, and un-
matched protocols. Connecting the research artifacts to Android also forced the class mapping, tensor
contract, and training-versus-inference boundary to be stated precisely.
The project would have been stronger with an experiment registry established before large-scale
screening. Every run should have frozen its dataset manifest, environment, command, checkpointhash,
prediction file, and evaluation report. Three-to-five-seed runs, consistent software environments, and
strict one-factor ablations would have made the causal interpretation of components more reliable.
Primary prediction files and confusion matrices should have been retained automatically. Independent
expert annotation review, a unified corruption benchmark, and timing instrumentation separated by
pipeline stage would have reduced the remaining scientific and deployment uncertainty.

3.1 Classification Limitations
The primary classification result uses one fixed seed and does not provide a distribution across re-
peated runs. Candidate screening inspected test-partition Macro-F1, so the model-family comparison
did not preserve a completely untouched selection-independent test. The final primary prediction file,
classwise report, and confusion matrices were not retained, which prevents exact error-transition anal-
ysis. The dataset is limited in size and imbalanced, and its acquisition conditions may contain source-
specific cues. Background removal can reduce irrelevant context but can also alter weak boundaries
or texture evidence.
ShrimpDB-3 and Combined-4 were retraining experiments rather than direct external inference.
They measure performance after adapting to the mapped data and therefore do not quantify out-of-
source generalization by the original checkpoint. A matched final-model profile comparing CE and
SimAM-DCFR under one inference script was also not retained, so the exact inference overhead of
the final feature-recalibration integration remains unresolved.
3.2 Segmentation Limitations
Specimen identity was inferred from filenames. This supports grouped splitting for documented
repeated views but may not detect undocumented duplicates, mislabeled identifiers, or acquisition cor-
relations. Masks were produced by one annotator without independent expert review or inter-annotator
agreement.
Ambiguous overlapping evidence was resolved through a deterministic project rule rather than
independently validated nested masks.
The attention experiments used heterogeneous augmentation policies, training budgets, early stop-
ping, initialization paths, framework versions, placements, and objectives. The combined segmen-
tation extension used a mixed grouped–stratified allocation because some additional images lacked
compatible specimen identifiers. Feature-domain Fourier refinement did not retain a final fully au-
dited result. The segmentation corruption summary contained selected positive-gain conditions, and
complete end-to-end deployment of the final segmentation checkpoint on Android was not proven.
3.3 Robustness and Explainability Limitations
Classification and segmentation used different corruption families and reporting rules. Their ro-
bustness values cannot be pooled or treated as one common benchmark. The transformations were
synthetic and do not reproduce all farm conditions, cameras, water properties, compression pipelines,
or disease stages. Complete per-severity machine-readable ledgers were not retained for every branch,
preventing unified severity curves and paired analyses.
Grad-CAM++ HiResCAM and EigenCAM emain qualitative. Heatmap agreement
may reveal obvious body-focused behavior, but it does not establish causal reliance on a disease sign.
Heatmaps are neither clinical evidence nor substitutes for independently reviewed segmentation
masks.
3.4 Deployment Limitations
The mobile evidence contains one bounded device observation for FP32 CPU inference with four
threads. No systematic GPU, NNAPI, or alternative LiteRT delegate benchmark was retained. The
displayed runtime was not separated into image decode, foreground preparation, tensor construction,
interpreter initialization, network execution, output decoding, and interface update. No cross-device
or thermal-stability study was conducted.

3.5 Prioritized Future Work
The next development cycle should follow a staged evidence-preservation plan:
1. Freeze dataset inventories, split manifests, checkpoints, prediction files, and environment hashes
for every reported run.
2. Use validation-only model selection and reserve an untouched final test partition.
3. Run three to five independent seeds and report the distribution of primary metrics.
4. Regenerate classwise reports, count and normalized confusion matrices, calibration analysis,
and paired failure cases from frozen predictions.
5. Conduct multi-annotator segmentation review, including independent expert inspection and in-
terannotator agreement.
6. Strengthen specimen identity with duplicate detection, acquisition metadata, and source-aware
grouping.
7. Perform strict matched attention and Fourier ablations under identical data, initialization, opti-
mization, augmentation, software, and checkpoint rules.
8. Evaluate complete common-corruption families and severities with a shared machine-readable
ledger for both tasks where technically appropriate.
9. Conduct true external inference and farm- or source-grouped evaluation without retraining the
original checkpoint.
10. Profile FP32 and quantized artifacts across CPU, GPU, NNAPI, and supported LiteRT delegates
on multiple Android devices.
11. Export and test the final selected segmentation checkpoint end to end, including tensor decoding,
mask reconstruction, overlay alignment, and regression cases.
12. Conduct a controlled usability study with defined tasks, failure scenarios, confidence communi-
cation, and user-comprehension measures.
13. Keep the system positioned as preliminary screening support until independent veterinary and
field validation exists.
The project establishes a traceable research and engineering foundation for shrimp-disease image
screening. Further progress depends on stronger data coverage, cleaner selection protocols, repeated-
run evidence, independent annotation review, unified robustness testing, and complete cross-device
deployment validation.
